Production specification

SourceHub Blueprint

SourceHub connects clients who need real-world data — imagery, video, sensor readings, people-based field work — with delivery partners who fulfil it through their own networks of crowd aggregators, vendor businesses and equipment sponsors. This document specifies the production system behind that marketplace: the domain, the architecture, the money, the trust posture and the interface.

Part one

The marketplace

Two things make SourceHub structurally different from a generic services marketplace, and almost every decision downstream falls out of them: the platform contracts with only two of the five parties, and work passes two independent quality gates before a client ever sees it.

Topology and money

SourceHub registers, bills and holds a contractual relationship with clients and delivery partners only. A partner's aggregators, business partners and device sponsors are that partner's own commercial relationships. They authenticate into SourceHub and do real work in it, but the platform never invoices them and never pays them.

Decision

The platform takes a 9% fee on contract value, deducted at payout to the delivery partner. Clients are invoiced the contract value; partners receive value minus fee.

Charging one side keeps the client's budget comparison clean — the number a partner bids is the number the client pays — while the fee sits where the margin is. It also keeps the platform out of the partner's own cost structure, which varies wildly between a 1,200-person crowd guild and a 40-crew vendor.

Decision

Supplier attribution is visible to the client by default. The delivery breakdown names the aggregator or business behind each task.

Clients buying field data are buying provenance; hiding the chain of custody undermines the audit trail they need for their own compliance. Partners who treat their network as commercially sensitive can switch a contract to anonymised attribution, which substitutes a stable pseudonym so lineage still holds.

Lifecycle and QA gates

A request moves through seven stages — Draft, Published, Proposals Received, Accepted, In Progress, Delivered, Completed — but the interesting mechanism is underneath, at task level, where captured data can be rejected at three different points by three different parties.

Decision

The client's acceptance criteria are captured as a machine-checkable rubric plus a sampling plan, not free text, from the moment the request is drafted.

"95% or better pass on the automated blur and occlusion check; 5% manual audit sample" is executable: it compiles to a threshold on an automated check and an ISO 2859-1 style sampling plan the partner's QA queue enforces. Free-text criteria are the root cause of acceptance disputes, and disputes are the most expensive thing that can happen on a data-sourcing contract.

Part two

The system

A modular monolith with an asynchronous media plane. The transactional side of this product is small — thousands of contracts, not millions — while the media side is genuinely large. Splitting along that line, rather than along org-chart lines, is the whole architectural argument.

Service decomposition

Decision

Ship a modular monolith for the transactional domain and a separately scaled media plane for ingest and processing. Do not start with microservices.

Requests, proposals, contracts and tasks are one tightly-coupled transactional cluster; splitting them across services buys distributed transactions and buys nothing else at this volume. Ingest is the opposite: bursty, CPU-heavy, failure-prone, and needs to scale on a completely different curve. Enforce module boundaries in code — one schema namespace per module, no cross-module table reads — so extraction stays cheap when a module earns it.

Tenancy and isolation

SourceHub has two independent tenancy axes, and conflating them is the most likely source of a data-leak bug in this product. The account axis separates one client from another and one partner from another. The network axis says an aggregator belongs to exactly one partner and must be invisible to every other partner — including partners bidding on the same request.

Decision

Shared database, shared schema, Postgres row-level security as the enforcement point — not application-layer filtering.

Application filters are one forgotten WHERE away from a cross-tenant leak, and this product has an unusually high number of list endpoints. RLS makes the safe path the default: a query that forgets the predicate returns nothing rather than everything. Enterprise clients with residency obligations get a dedicated schema in their region; the code path is identical because the predicate is in the database either way.

Known risk

RLS predicates are invisible in application code, so a developer reading a repository method cannot see why a row is missing.

Mitigation: a test harness that runs every list endpoint under three contexts — owner, sibling org, unrelated org — and fails the build if a sibling or unrelated context sees a single row. This is cheap and catches the class of bug that would otherwise reach production.

Capture to delivery

The media plane is where the volume lives: a single 900-hour dashcam contract is tens of terabytes, arriving over weeks from sixty drivers on unreliable connections.

Decision

Privacy redaction runs on the capture device, and the server verifies a sample rather than performing the redaction itself.

For a GDPR request like the dashcam contract, doing redaction server-side means unredacted footage of identifiable faces and plates is transmitted, stored and processed — creating exactly the liability the requirement exists to avoid. Verifying a sample keeps the device honest without ever holding the raw data. The trade-off is a heavier capture app and a hardware floor, which belongs in the partner's proposal.

API and events

REST over HTTPS, /v1 in the path, cursor pagination, Idempotency-Key required on every unsafe method, RFC 9457 problem details for errors. Webhooks are signed and replayable.

Decision

The audit trail is derived from the same event stream the webhooks publish, not written separately by each handler.

An audit log maintained by hand drifts from reality the first time someone adds a code path and forgets the log line. Deriving it from events that already have to exist for notifications means the trail is complete by construction — and every "who changed this and when" question a client's compliance team asks has an answer.

Identity and access

OIDC for platform accounts, SAML 2.0 and SCIM for enterprise clients and partners, TOTP or WebAuthn as a second factor, enforced for any role that can move money or approve a delivery. Aggregator crowd workers authenticate through their aggregator, not through the platform.

Payments and payouts

  • On award — milestone 1 (50% by default, negotiable per contract) is invoiced to the client and held in escrow. The contract cannot start until it clears.
  • On acceptance — escrow releases, the 9% platform fee is deducted, and the balance is queued for payout to the partner.
  • On dispute — escrow is frozen. Ops arbitrates against the rubric snapshot taken at award, which is why the rubric must be immutable from that moment.
  • Never — the platform does not pay aggregators, businesses, sponsors or crowd workers. That settlement is the partner's, off-platform.

KYB and payout rails through a marketplace payment provider (Stripe Connect or Adyen for Platforms), which also carries sanctions screening. Tax handling covers W-8/W-9 collection, VAT and GST determination by client region, and DAC7 reporting for EU-resident partners — a marketplace reporting obligation that is easy to discover far too late.

Known risk

Escrow makes SourceHub look like a money transmitter in several jurisdictions.

Mitigation: hold funds through the payment provider's regulated escrow product rather than in a platform-controlled account, and take a written legal opinion per launch market before the first contract is awarded there. This gates market expansion, not the build.

Security and compliance

Certifications and frameworks

  • SOC 2 Type II — target within 12 months of general availability
  • ISO/IEC 27001 — follows SOC 2, driven by EU enterprise demand
  • GDPR — DPA, standard contractual clauses, DPIA for any capture involving identifiable people
  • HIPAA — BAA and a segregated processing path for clinical categories
  • CCPA/CPRA — consumer requests routed through the client of record

Controls

  • Envelope encryption at rest with per-tenant data keys; TLS 1.3 in transit
  • Pre-signed URLs scoped to one object with a 15-minute TTL
  • Hash-chained audit log; tampering is detectable, not merely discouraged
  • Annual third-party penetration test; critical findings fixed within 7 days
  • Least-privilege service accounts; no standing production data access for engineers

Obligations specific to this product

  • Consent capture — people-based deliverables carry a consent artefact per subject, stored with the asset and surviving deletion of the asset itself
  • Data residency — object storage and the processing schema are pinned per client region; a task cannot be assigned to a supplier outside the permitted region
  • Right to erasure — propagates through derived assets and delivered bundles, with a documented 30-day window and a tombstone in the chain of custody
  • Retention — default 24 months post-contract, configurable down per client, with legal-hold override
  • Fair work — partners attest to pay-rate transparency and a minimum effective hourly rate for crowd work. The platform cannot enforce employment terms it is not party to, but it can and does make attestation a condition of listing.

Part three

The interface

Six roles, one product. The interface has to be legible to a procurement lead at a retail analytics firm and to an operations manager running 820 crowd workers, without becoming two products. The design system is what holds that together, and it ships as running code rather than a picture of code.

Tokens and taxonomy

Every colour, size and duration in the product is a CSS custom property. Components read tokens and nothing else, which is what makes the dark theme a swap of values rather than a second stylesheet.

The palette

Neutrals carry a slight green bias so they read as chosen rather than inherited. The accent is a petrol teal drawn from survey instrumentation — the world this product actually operates in.

accent · petrol teal ground · #f4f6f5 ink · #0f1a1c

Type

IBM Plex Sans for interface, IBM Plex Mono for every identifier, metric and uppercase label. Monospacing the identifiers is not decoration: RFP-1001 and CTR-01 are read, compared and quoted into support tickets all day, and tabular figures make columns of money and counts scan vertically.

The status taxonomy

Five tones, deliberately held apart from the accent so state never competes with navigation. Every status string in the product maps to exactly one tone and one owner — the party who must act next.

neutral · inert active · progressing attention · waiting on a human success · terminal, good critical · terminal, bad

The owner field is the load-bearing half. It generates the "waiting on" column, each role's personal queue, and the escalation rules — which is why a status can never be invented at the call site.

Decision

The design system documentation page is generated from the running product's own code, and reachable from inside every role.

A design system maintained as a separate artefact is out of date the week after it ships. Rendering the swatches from the live tokens and the pills from the live status table means the documentation cannot drift: if a token changes, the page changes, and if someone adds a status without a tone the page shows the gap immediately.

Layout

A 264px navigation rail collapses to a 64px icon rail below 1180px and becomes an off-canvas drawer below 840px. Content is capped at 1440px. Wide content — tables, diagrams — scrolls inside its own container so the page body never scrolls sideways. Spacing comes from a 4/8/12/16/24/32/48 scale with no arbitrary values; radii are 4px for controls and 6px for panels, with the pill radius reserved exclusively for status. Surfaces are flat with hairline borders and shadow is reserved for things that genuinely float, which is what keeps a dense operations table readable at 11px row padding.

Internationalisation

Supplier-facing surfaces need Hindi, Tagalog, Bahasa, Swahili and Portuguese at launch — that is where the crowd is. All strings externalised, ICU message format for plurals and gender, logical CSS properties throughout so an RTL locale is a dir attribute rather than a stylesheet fork, and dates rendered in the reader's locale while identifiers stay in Latin script.

Part four

Getting there

Four phases. Each one ends with something a real client and a real partner can complete a transaction on, because a marketplace that cannot close a loop teaches you nothing.

Delivery phases

Phase 1

Close one loop

Months 1–3
  • Identity, orgs, RBAC, RLS with the isolation test harness
  • Requests, proposals, award, contracts, tasks
  • Manual QA at both gates, no automation
  • Invoicing only — no escrow, settlement handled by finance
  • Two design partners, one category, one region
Phase 2

Make the media real

Months 4–7
  • Capture app, pre-signed multipart upload, resumable transfer
  • Worker pool: checksum, scan, metadata, transcode
  • Automated quality checks and the rubric compiler
  • Sampling plans and gold sets
  • Escrow through the payment provider; DAC7 groundwork
Phase 3

Earn the enterprise

Months 8–12
  • SAML, SCIM, audit export, per-region residency
  • SOC 2 Type II evidence collection and audit
  • Public API, signed webhooks, partner sandbox
  • Dispute arbitration workflow and ops console
  • Supplier-facing localisation
Phase 4

Make the market liquid

Year 2
  • Partner matching and ranking on delivered outcomes, not self-description
  • Reusable capability profiles so partners stop retyping their method
  • Forward capacity: partners publish availability, clients book against it
  • Benchmark pricing from settled contracts
